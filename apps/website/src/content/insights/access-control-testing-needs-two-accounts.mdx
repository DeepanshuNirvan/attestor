Broken access control has been number one on the OWASP Top 10 since 2021 and it stayed there in the
2025 edition. It is also the class that automated scanning is worst at, for a reason that is
structural rather than a gap somebody will close: a scanner cannot know that order 1042 belongs to a
different customer than the one whose session it is holding.

A tester can know that, but only if there are two customers.

## The two directions

Access control fails in two directions and they need different setups.

**Vertical** is one role reaching another role's functionality. Support reaching the admin export.
A customer reaching the support console. This one needs one account per role and most clients do
provide it.

**Horizontal** is one user reaching another user's data inside the same role. Customer A reading
customer B's invoices. This is the one that produces the finding your enterprise buyer cares about,
because it is the one that means "any of your customers can read any other customer's data", and it
is impossible to test with one account per role.

You cannot substitute. Testing horizontal access control by guessing identifiers with a single
account gives you either a 404 you cannot interpret or a 200 you cannot prove was somebody else's
data. With two accounts you have ground truth: this identifier belongs to B, A requested it, A got
it, here is the screenshot of both sides.

## What the replay matrix actually does

During authenticated crawling we record every request each role makes, with the identifiers that
appeared in each response. Then, for every recorded request from role A:

- replay it with role B's session
- replay it with an unauthenticated session
- replay it with identifier fields mutated to values captured from the other account
- replay it with identifiers of adjacent shape, so sequential enumeration is visible

Then compare. Not for exact equality — no two responses are byte-identical when there is a timestamp
and a CSRF token in them — but structurally: status, length band, shape of the JSON, presence of the
key fields. Anything where a lower privilege receives substantially the same content as a higher one
is a candidate.

**Candidate**, not finding. The comparison heuristic produces false positives, particularly on
endpoints that legitimately return the same public data to everyone. A human confirms each one and,
more importantly, writes what it means. "The endpoint returns another user's record" is a technical
statement. "Any registered customer can retrieve the delivery address and phone number of any other
customer by counting upwards" is the sentence that gets the fix prioritised.

## The multi-tenant case is worse

If you sell to businesses, the accounts you need are in two separate customer organisations, not two
users in one. Tenant scoping is usually applied in the main read path and forgotten in three others:
the search index, the export, and the webhook. Those three are written at different times by
different people and each one is a fresh chance to filter after retrieval rather than during it.

Testing that needs an account in tenant one and an account in tenant two. There is no way around it,
and if you cannot provide it, the report will say the tenant isolation check was not performed and
why. That sentence in the coverage matrix is worse for you than the cost of creating two accounts.

## What we ask for now

Two accounts per role, disposable, created for the engagement. For a typical application with
customer, admin and support roles that is six accounts. If it is multi-tenant, two organisations with
two accounts each in the roles that matter.

It takes about ten minutes to create. It is the difference between a report that says "we tested
access control" and one that says which customer could read which other customer's data.
